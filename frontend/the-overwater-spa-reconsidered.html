Wellness

The overwater spa, reconsidered

By Camille Ardent · 28 May 2026 · 7 min read

The overwater spa is one of travel's great images: a glass floor, a lagoon moving underneath, a treatment room suspended over turquoise. It photographs beautifully. It is also, on its own, close to useless — and the gap between the image and the outcome is where most wellness weeks quietly fail.

The failure is structural. A spa week is usually sold as a menu: pick eight treatments, distribute them across seven days, arrive home tired in a slightly different way. Nothing in that structure is designed to produce rest. It is designed to produce bookings.

Rest is a schedule problem

Genuine restoration turns out to be less about what you do and more about what the week does to your nervous system. That means a shape, not a list. The pattern we now build looks roughly like this:

  • Days one and two: subtract. No treatments at all. Sleep badly, then sleep well. Let the travel leave your body before you start doing anything to it.
  • Days three to five: one thing a day. A single appointment, always in the morning, and nothing scheduled after it. The afternoon is the treatment.
  • Days six and seven: build. Movement, water, something mildly demanding — a long swim, a walk with height in it — so you return with momentum rather than inertia.
The massage is not the intervention. The empty afternoon after it is.

What to ask a spa before you commit

Two questions separate the serious from the decorative. First: is there a practitioner who sees me more than once, and do they talk to each other? A week of eight strangers each performing sixty minutes is a sequence of transactions, not a programme. Second: what happens at night? Sleep is the entire mechanism, and a property that has thought about blackout, temperature, sound and a genuinely early dinner is doing more for you than any treatment room over water.

The case for the water, after all

None of which is an argument against the lagoon. Cold water in particular has earned its reputation — a sea swim before breakfast does something no table can. And there is a real, unmeasurable benefit to a room where the floor moves with the tide and the only sound at three in the morning is water against timber.

Keep the overwater spa. Just stop treating it as the plan. The plan is the emptiness around it, and emptiness, it turns out, is the hardest thing on earth to book.
