Slow Travel

Shoulder season is the new peak

By Camille Ardent · 18 July 2026 · 6 min read

There is a particular kind of traveler who has stopped asking when a place is at its best and started asking when it is at its quietest. For them, the calendar has inverted. The weeks everyone else calls peak — the crowded, sun-blanched heart of summer — have become the season to avoid. The margins, the shoulders, the almost-seasons on either side: that is where the good travelling now happens.

It is easy to mistake this for thrift. It is not. Shoulder-season travel can cost as much as any other; what it buys is different. Not a discount, but a dividend — of light, of space, of the sense that a place is being itself rather than performing for a crowd.

What the crowds take with them

Visit the Amalfi Coast in early June or late September and you meet a different coast entirely. The same cliffs, the same lemon terraces, but the roads move, the restaurants have tables, and the light — lower, softer, kinder to both the landscape and the photographs — arrives without the flattening glare of August noon.

The luxury is not that it is cheaper. The luxury is that it is emptier.

The staff are different too. In the off-months they have time; the concierge who is triaging four hundred guests in July can, in October, actually plan your day. Service, that most quoted of luxuries, is largely a function of ratio. Improve the ratio and you improve everything downstream of it.

The strategy, in practice

Planning for shoulder season is less about the destination than the timing, and timing rewards a little discipline. A few principles we return to again and again:

  • Chase the fortnight, not the month. Every destination has a narrow window where weather still holds but crowds have gone. It is often only two weeks wide. Find it, and build around it.
  • Read the weather like a local, not a brochure. "Rainy season" rarely means all-day rain; it often means a spectacular afternoon hour and then clear, washed light. The brochures never tell you this.
  • Let the calendar flex. The best shoulder trips move by a week when the forecast asks them to. Book the kind of stays that let you.

Where we're sending people this year

Kyoto in late November, once the tour buses thin and the maples turn. The Cyclades in the first fortnight of October, the sea still warm, the ferries half empty. The Alps in the green shoulder of early summer, before the season proper, when the valleys are yours and the spas are unhurried.

None of these are secrets, exactly. They are simply the same beloved places, met a few degrees off the crowd's timing — which turns out to make all the difference. Peak was never the best time to go. It was only the most obvious.
