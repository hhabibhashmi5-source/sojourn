Design

Rooms that ask nothing of you

By Idris Vale · 12 June 2026 · 5 min read

There was a decade — we all lived through it — when hotel rooms shouted. Feature walls. Statement bathtubs placed, inexplicably, at the foot of the bed. Lighting systems requiring a tutorial. Every surface working hard to be photographed, and the guest reduced to an extra in someone else's portfolio.

That decade is ending, and what is replacing it is harder to do and much easier to live in.

The test is the second morning

A room reveals itself not on arrival but on the second morning, when the novelty has gone and you simply need to find your things, make a coffee and sit somewhere comfortable to read. Rooms designed for the arrival photograph rarely survive this. Rooms designed for the second morning almost always do.

A good room does not introduce itself. You notice it three days later, by the absence of irritation.

The details that matter turn out to be dull to photograph and expensive to get right: a bedside light you can dim with one hand in the dark, a wardrobe that opens without a manual, a desk at the correct height, a bathroom where the mirror does not fog, sockets on both sides of the bed, and a door that closes with a sound rather than a bang.

Restraint is not minimalism

This is worth separating, because they are often confused. Minimalism is a look — white, hard, sparse, and frequently as demanding as the maximalism it replaced. Restraint is a discipline: the room may be warm, layered, full of texture and books and worn leather, but nothing in it is asking for your attention.

The best examples we know are often the least fashionable: an old ryokan room with nothing in it but tatami, a scroll and a view; a Provençal farmhouse bedroom with lime-washed walls and a chair by the window; a fjord cabin that is essentially a well-made box aimed at the water. None of them are trying. All of them work.

What this means when you book

Look past the hero shot. Ask for a photograph of the desk, the wardrobe, the actual light at 7pm. Ask what the room sounds like — from the corridor, from the street, from the room above. Ask whether the windows open, which sounds absurd until the third night in a sealed box.

The rooms worth returning to are rarely the ones that impressed you. They are the ones that let you stop noticing the room at all, which is, in the end, the whole point of somewhere to sleep.
