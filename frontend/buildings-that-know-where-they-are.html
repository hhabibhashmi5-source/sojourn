Buildings that know where they are

By Camille Ardent · 30 April 2026 · 6 min read

There is a hotel room that exists in forty countries. Beige, competent, a padded headboard, a print of something abstract, a bathroom in grey stone. It is not bad. It is simply from nowhere, and waking in it tells you nothing about where you are.

The alternative is not folklore. Nobody wants a themed room. What they want, usually without being able to name it, is a building made from the assumptions of its own place.

What local actually means

It is rarely decorative and almost always structural. Thick walls and small windows in hot dry places, because that is how you stay cool without machinery. Deep eaves where it rains. South-facing glass and a stove where it is cold. Courtyards where the street is loud. Every one of these is a response to a specific problem, and buildings that solve local problems end up looking local without trying.

Vernacular architecture is not a style. It is several centuries of people finding out what works here.

The material test

  • Where did the stone come from? A wall of local limestone and a wall of imported travertine cost similar amounts and mean entirely different things.
  • Who made the furniture? Not the brand — the workshop. The good properties can name it, and it is usually within two hours.
  • Does the building have a shape it could not have elsewhere? A riad only makes sense in a hot walled city. A fjord cabin only makes sense on a fjord. If the form would transplant anywhere, it came from nowhere.

Why it changes the week

Because a building that answers its own climate also teaches you the climate. You learn why the shutters close at noon in Provence by living in a house that expects you to. You understand a Japanese room's relationship to its garden by sitting in one at the hour it was designed for.

That is the actual luxury on offer — not the thread count, but waking up somewhere that could only be here.
