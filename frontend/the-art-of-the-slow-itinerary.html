Slow Travel

The art of the slow itinerary

By Camille Ardent · 3 April 2026 · 5 min read

Ask someone about a trip six months later and watch what they actually remember. Almost never the itinerary. Almost always an afternoon that was not on it — a wrong turn, a long lunch that ran into evening, an hour on a wall watching a harbour do nothing in particular.

We have been planning against this for years without noticing. The proposal that looks generous — six things a day, nothing missed — is the one that guarantees none of those afternoons can happen. A full itinerary is not a rich trip. It is a trip with no room in it.

The half rule

The change we made is embarrassingly simple: one fixed thing per day, in the morning, and nothing else committed. Not fewer good experiences — the same ones, with space around them. A morning at a vineyard becomes a morning at a vineyard and whatever the vineyard suggests over lunch, which is usually better than anything we would have booked.

Nobody has ever come home and said the problem was that they saw too little.

What the empty hours are for

  • Repetition. Going back to the same café three mornings running is how a place stops being scenery. It cannot happen on a full schedule.
  • Weather. An unplanned afternoon absorbs rain without ruining anything. A planned one turns rain into a loss.
  • The local suggestion. The best recommendations arrive at eleven in the morning from someone you have just met, and are useless if two o'clock is spoken for.
  • Actual rest. Which is, for most people, the stated reason for the trip and the first thing the itinerary removes.

How to hold the line

The pressure to fill a day is real, and it usually comes from the traveler rather than the planner — a day with nothing in it looks, on paper, like money wasted. It is worth resisting for three days, which is roughly how long it takes for the discomfort to convert into relief.

It helps to move less. Two bases in ten nights rather than four. Every transfer costs half a day at each end and buys a fresh set of orientation problems. It also helps to book stays you would happily spend an afternoon inside, because on a slow itinerary you will.

The result reads thin in a document and lands rich in memory. That trade — a less impressive plan for a better week — has become the single most useful thing we do.
