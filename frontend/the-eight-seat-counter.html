Gastronomy

The eight-seat counter

By Camille Ardent · 10 March 2026 · 6 min read

The most interesting restaurants in the world have been getting smaller for a decade. Not cheaper, not simpler — smaller. Eight seats, ten, sometimes six, one service, one sitting, frequently no menu and increasingly no public booking system at all.

It is easy to read this as artificial scarcity. Sometimes it is. Mostly it is a chef solving a real problem.

What the counter is actually for

At eight seats, one person can cook and plate every dish at the moment it is ready and hand it directly to the person eating it. Nothing waits under a pass, nothing crosses a dining room, and nothing is compromised to be reproducible forty times a night. The food is simply better, and it is better for structural reasons rather than aspirational ones.

Eight seats is not exclusivity. It is the largest number of people one person can cook for properly.

The second thing it buys is information. The chef can see you eat. Courses get adjusted mid-service based on how the room is doing — lighter, slower, an extra one because the table is enjoying itself. That responsiveness disappears completely at forty covers.

The awkward part

Small rooms are intimate, which is wonderful with the right company and genuinely difficult with the wrong. You will hear the next conversation. You cannot leave early without it being noticed. If you want a private evening, a counter is the wrong choice, and the honest advice is a larger room with better spacing.

How they are actually booked

  • Through the property, weeks ahead. A good hotel in the city holds relationships that no platform has. This is most of what a concierge is now for.
  • By being flexible on the night. Two people on a Tuesday get in where four on a Saturday never will.
  • By turning up at opening. Many keep two seats back and give them to whoever is standing there at six.
  • By eating early. The first sitting is frequently the better one — the kitchen is fresh and the pacing is not compressed.

None of this requires influence. It requires planning three weeks earlier than feels necessary, which is, as usual, the entire secret.
