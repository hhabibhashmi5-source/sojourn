Kyoto before the crowds

By Noor Haddad · 22 April 2026 · 9 min read

Kyoto has a reputation problem, and it is entirely the fault of the middle of the day. Between ten and four, the famous sites are a slow-moving queue with a temple attached, and every photograph you have ever admired of the city becomes visibly impossible.

The city has not changed. The hours have. Almost everything people love about Kyoto is still there, and most of it is available before eight in the morning to anyone willing to set an alarm.

The ninety-minute advantage

Fushimi Inari is the clearest example. By eleven the lower gates are a shuffling column. At six they are silent, the light comes through the vermilion in bars, and you can walk twenty minutes up the mountain without meeting anyone. The site did not improve; you simply arrived before the coaches.

The difference between Kyoto as a queue and Kyoto as a revelation is about ninety minutes of sleep.

The same is true of the moss gardens, of Kiyomizu's approach, of the bamboo at Arashiyama — which is genuinely magnificent at dawn and genuinely miserable at noon. Build the week around early mornings and unstructured afternoons and the city rearranges itself around you.

What a guide is actually for

Not narration. You can read. A good Kyoto guide is a set of relationships: the sub-temple that will open a hall for four people, the tea practitioner who does not take visitors, the indigo dyer in the fifth generation, the counter with eight seats that has never had a listing. These cannot be booked. They can only be introduced.

Our fixer there has spent fifteen years on this, and the difference it makes is not access to more things — it is access to fewer things, properly. An hour with a knife-maker who explains why the steel is folded is worth a day of temple exteriors.

When to go, and when not to

  • Late November for the maples, but the last week rather than the first — the crowds follow the forecast by a few days.
  • Early March, before the blossom, when the city is cold, clear and almost empty. Our quiet favourite.
  • Avoid the first fortnight of April unless cherry blossom is the entire point, and even then, plan every morning for six o'clock.
  • Avoid Golden Week altogether. Domestic travel fills everything, and no amount of introduction fixes it.

The afternoon rule

Having earned the mornings, do nothing with the afternoons. Kyoto punishes ambition — the city is spread out, the transit is slow, and three sites in a day means three queues and a taxi argument. One thing before breakfast, lunch somewhere small, and then a bath, a book and a walk in whichever direction looks quiet.

It is not an efficient way to see a city. It is an extremely good way to know one.
